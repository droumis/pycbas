CBAS Correlative Mode

When you don't have discrete groups, but a continuous measure per subject.

0 When to Use Correlative Mode

The comparative walkthrough (Part 1) asks: "Do these two groups produce different behavioral patterns?" But sometimes you don't have two clean groups. Instead, you have a continuous measurement for each subject, such as a clinical score, a performance index, or an age.

Correlative mode asks: "Are any behavioral patterns correlated with this continuous measure?" For example, in humans performing a 6-armed bandit task, each subject has a CBIT score (a measure of cognitive flexibility). CBAS can find which choice sequences are produced more or less by subjects with higher CBIT scores.

Comparative mode

Input: subjects split into 2 groups

Question: which sequences differ between groups?

Stat: studentized difference in means

Null: shuffle group labels

Correlative mode

Input: each subject has a continuous score

Question: which sequences correlate with the score?

Stat: studentized Pearson correlation

Null: permute the scores across subjects

Steps 1 (counting sequences), 4 (step-down), and 5 (k-FWER) work identically. The differences are in how you measure the effect (step 2) and how you build the null (step 3).

1 Measuring Correlation

In comparative mode, you ask "do the group means differ?" In correlative mode, you ask "does this sequence's count go up (or down) as the covariate increases?"

This is a correlation question. Each subject is a data point with two values: how often they produced this sequence (x-axis) and their covariate score (y-axis). If there's a pattern where subjects who use this sequence more also have higher scores, that's a positive correlation.

The Pearson correlation (rho)

The Pearson correlation coefficient measures linear association between two variables. It ranges from -1 (perfect negative) through 0 (no relationship) to +1 (perfect positive).

ρ = Σ(Xi − X̄)(Yi − Ȳ) / √[Σ(Xi − X̄)² · Σ(Yi − Ȳ)²] Multiply each subject's deviation from the X mean by their deviation from the Y mean. If high-X subjects tend to also be high-Y, the products are mostly positive, giving a positive rho.

Why not just use rho directly?

A rho of 0.3 might be very significant with 1,000 subjects but meaningless with 10. Just like in comparative mode, you need to divide by a measure of uncertainty. The raw correlation needs to be "studentized" to account for sample size and variability.

The studentized correlation

CBAS uses a robust variance estimator called tau (from DiCiccio & Romano 2017) rather than the classical formula. This handles non-normal distributions more gracefully.

Building the test statistic step by step

1. Compute rho (the Pearson correlation between sequence counts and covariate).

2. Compute tau (the robust standard error). This measures how variable the correlation estimate is, accounting for heteroscedasticity (uneven spread).

3. Divide: t = √n × rho / tau

The result is a t-value. Larger absolute values mean more evidence that the correlation is real rather than noise.

t = √n · ρ / τ where τ = √[mean((Xi−X̄)²(Yi−Ȳ)²)] / [std(X) · std(Y)]. This is more robust than the classical √(n-2) · r / √(1-r²) formula.

The bar shows the t-value computed from the scatter plot above. The color indicates direction: blue for positive correlation, pink for negative. As you increase the correlation or decrease noise, the t-value grows.

Two one-sided tests (same as comparative mode)

Just like before, CBAS stores the t-value in one of two slots depending on direction. If rho > 0, the t goes in the "positive" slot. If rho < 0, the absolute value goes in the "negative" slot. This doubles the hypothesis count to 2S.

2 Building the Null by Permutation

In comparative mode, the null asks "what if group membership were random?" by reshuffling who belongs to which group. In correlative mode, the analogous question is "what if the relationship between behavior and the covariate were random?"

The answer: permute the covariate. Keep everyone's behavioral sequences exactly as they are, but randomly reassign the scores. Subject A keeps their choice stream but gets subject B's CBIT score. Then recompute correlations for all sequences.

0 / 10,000

The left panel shows the scatter plot. When you permute, the y-values (covariate scores) get shuffled across the x-values (sequence counts). The correlation mostly vanishes. The right panel accumulates the t-value from each permutation, building up the null distribution.

Why permutation instead of resampling? In correlative mode, you want to break the link between behavior and the covariate while preserving the marginal distributions of both. Permutation does exactly this: every subject's count stays the same, every score stays in the pool, but the pairing is randomized.

Same matrix structure as before

After 10,000 permutations, you have the same matrix as in comparative mode: 10,000 rows (one per permutation) and one column per sequence-direction test. Each cell is "what this sequence's t-value would have been if behavior and the covariate were unrelated." The step-down and k-FWER procedures then operate on this matrix identically to the comparative case.

3 Everything Else is the Same

Once you have the null matrix, the rest of the pipeline is identical to comparative mode:

  • Step-down: sort observed t-values, compare against row-maximums (or k-th largest) of the null matrix, peel off rejected sequences, threshold drops.
  • k-FWER: iterate to find the k where the false discovery proportion is under control.
  • Output: ζ-values per sequence, direction of correlation (positive or negative), the converged k.

The only differences are upstream: how you compute the test statistic and how you generate the null. Everything downstream of the null matrix is mode-agnostic.

Think of it like two different ways to fill a swimming pool. Comparative mode fills it with group-shuffled water. Correlative mode fills it with score-permuted water. Once the pool is full, you swim in it the same way (step-down, k-FWER, significance calls).

4 Interpreting Correlative Results

A significant sequence in correlative mode means: "Subjects who produce this pattern more (or less) tend to have higher (or lower) covariate scores, and this relationship is stronger than what you'd expect from 10,000 random score reassignments, even after correcting for testing thousands of sequences."

Example from the human dataset

With 1,413 human subjects performing a 6-armed bandit task, each subject has a CBIT score. CBAS finds 31 sequences (out of 408 tested) that are significantly correlated with CBIT, at k=2. Some sequences correlate positively (subjects with higher CBIT scores produce them more) and some negatively.

This tells you something about the structure of cognitive flexibility: it's not just that flexible people do better, but that they produce specific identifiable patterns of choices at specific lengths.

Direction matters

Just like in comparative mode, the output tells you the direction. A positive correlation means "more of this pattern goes with higher scores." A negative correlation means "less of this pattern goes with higher scores." The algorithm tests both directions independently for each sequence.
